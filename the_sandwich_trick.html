A plain-words guide for high-school readers

The Sandwich Trick

How to be completely sure about something you can only measure fuzzily, and how the same idea, pushed all the way, replaced the safety guarantees in the Cyclecraft machine study with exact values, some of them 60,000 times smaller.

A box you are not allowed to open

Picture a ruler that runs from $-1$ to $1$. Somewhere along it sit a few marbles of different weights. You cannot see them. What you can do is ask the box for certain averages: the total weight, the weighted average position, the weighted average of position squared, and so on, up to 16 such questions. Mathematicians call these answers moments.

This is not a made-up puzzle. When an astronomer splits starlight into colours, the bright lines sit at particular frequencies with particular strengths, and the instrument often reports smoothed averages rather than the lines themselves. The same shape of problem shows up in medical scanners, in vibration sensors on bridges, and in simplified models of physical systems. The marbles are the hidden lines; the moments are what the instrument reports.

The catch is that real instruments are never exact. Every answer comes with an error bar: the true average is within, say, one millionth of what the instrument says. The question is what you can still say with certainty. Where are the marbles? How heavy is each one? And could a marble be hiding somewhere you did not look?

Step 1: a quantity that can never be negative

The whole method rests on one fact you already know: a square is never negative. Pick any polynomial $p(x)$, for example one that is zero exactly at your guesses for the marble positions. Then $p(x)^2$ is zero at those guesses and positive everywhere else.

Now take the weighted average of $p(x)^2$ over the marbles. You can calculate it directly from the moments, because $p(x)^2$ is just a combination of $1, x, x^2, \dots$, so its average is the same combination of the measured averages. If your guesses are exactly right, every marble sits where $p^2=0$, and the average is $0$. If a marble sits somewhere else, it adds a positive amount, and the average cannot be $0$.

The class-test version

Suppose every score on a test is at least $0$, and every student who missed the review session scored at least $10$. If the class average is $0.5$, then at most $5\%$ of the class can have missed the review: those students alone would push the average up by at least $10$ times their share. Our version reads the same way. Every value of $p^2$ is at least $0$, every marble far from your guesses gives at least some amount $g$, so the weight far from your guesses is at most (average of $p^2$) divided by $g$.

That already gives a guarantee, but a loose one. On our test box, with 4 marbles and measurements good to one millionth, it says at most $0.0066$ of the weight can be hiding away from the 4 recovered positions. The true answer is $0$. The bound is safe, but it is about 370 times larger than it needs to be.

Step 2: lay a blanket over the question

The square was one particular curve. Any curve works if it obeys 2 rules. It must never dip below $0$ anywhere on the ruler. And it must sit at height $1$ or higher over the region you are asking about. Picture a blanket thrown over a step: flat on the floor where the step is not, lifted to height $1$ over the step.

Here is why the blanket helps. Wherever there is a marble, the blanket is at least as high as the step. So the average height of the blanket is at least the average height of the step, and the average height of the step is exactly the share of weight in the band. Because the blanket is a polynomial, its average comes straight from the measured moments, with the error bars added in the unfavourable direction. That gives a guaranteed upper limit.

Different blankets give different limits, so the computer searches for the blanket with the smallest average. That search is a linear program, the same kind of optimisation airlines use to schedule crews. A blanket that also hugs the floor tightly at every marble gives a far better answer than the square did. On the test box the limit drops from $0.0066$ to $0.0000178$.

Step 3: never trust the computer's rounding

Computers normally store numbers with about 16 significant digits and round everything else. A blanket that looks above $0$ on screen might dip to $-0.000000000001$ between two plotted points, and then the guarantee is false. So the method never takes the search result on trust. It converts the blanket into exact fractions and lifts it by a tiny constant if needed. It then proves the 2 rules with an algorithm from 1829 called Sturm's theorem, which counts how many times a polynomial crosses zero in an interval without ever computing where. If the count is $0$ and the curve is positive at one end, it is positive everywhere in between. All of this runs in exact fraction arithmetic, so no rounding can sneak in. We call a result checked this way certified.

Step 4: squeeze from both sides

A blanket gives a ceiling. A curve slid under the step, one that stays at or below $1$ over the band and at or below $0$ everywhere else, gives a floor in the same way. With a ceiling and a floor, the answer is sandwiched. That is where the name comes from.

There is a third ingredient. The computer also builds an actual pretend arrangement of marbles that matches every measurement within its error bar, checked again with exact fractions. If that pretend arrangement puts $0.0000172$ of its weight outside the safe zones, then no method using only these measurements could ever promise less than $0.0000172$. Our ceiling was $0.0000178$. The best possible answer is therefore pinned inside a window about $3\%$ wide.

What the sandwich can do

Catch a marble you forgot. We hid a 5th marble weighing one thousandth of the total and then fitted only 4 marbles, as if we did not know about it. The fitted picture looked fine. But the floor curve proved that between $0.000999$ and $0.001007$ of the weight sits away from the 4 fitted positions. The true value is $0.001$. So the method did not just suspect a mistake; it proved one and measured how much was missing.

Weigh each marble. Putting a floor and a ceiling around each marble's neighbourhood gives its weight to within about $0.00002$.

Tell you which measurement to improve. The numbers that make up the best blanket say how much each measurement's error bar costs. Re-measuring the 4 most costly averages 10 times more precisely cut the ceiling by $86\%$. Re-measuring 4 chosen at random cut it by only $3\%$. The useful ones were not simply the high-order averages: the total weight came first.

A pattern we can see but have not proved. Make the measurements 100 times more precise, and the smallest zone around each marble that is guaranteed to contain weight shrinks about 10 times. In every case we tried, this square-root rule held to within a few percent. We have a rough explanation but no proof yet, so it stays labelled open.

Part two: the Cyclecraft machine

Cyclecraft is a separate study of a tiny model machine. It has 5 states and hops between them at random, like a molecule bumping around, while a repeating rhythm nudges it so that on average it travels around a loop in one direction. Think of a revolving door being pushed by a crowd that mostly, but not always, walks the same way. The number that matters is the net count $N$: how many times the machine crossed one particular doorway forwards minus backwards during 10 rhythm cycles. On average $N$ is about $4.6$. The risk is a bad run in which $N$ is $0$ or less, meaning the machine made no forward progress at all.

The Cyclecraft authors had already computed 7 facts about $N$ with extreme precision, to about 80 decimal places. These were the average of $N$, how spread out $N$ is, and 4 tilted averages: the average of $z^N$ for $z = \tfrac12, \tfrac23, \tfrac34, \tfrac78$.

Their bound was already a blanket

Take $z=\tfrac12$. Whenever $N$ is $0$ or negative, $(\tfrac12)^N$ is $1$ or more, and it is always positive. So $(\tfrac12)^N$ is a blanket over the bad-run step, and its average, which they had computed, caps the bad-run chance. That gave their published figure of $9.19\%$. It used 1 of the 7 facts.

Our method mixes all 7 into the best possible blanket. A blanket here is a combination of $1$, $N$, $N^2$ and the 4 tilted terms. Two problems are new compared with the marble box. First, $N$ is a whole number that can in principle be anything from minus infinity to plus infinity, so the computer cannot check every value. We proved 2 small results about how the blanket behaves far out in each direction, and the computer only has to check a finite stretch in the middle. Second, every one of these quantities takes fraction values at whole numbers, so this time the whole search runs in exact fractions from start to finish.

From the same 7 facts, the guarantee drops from $9.19\%$ to $2.76\%$. And this is not just a better number but the best possible one. The computer also built a pretend probability distribution for $N$ that matches all 7 facts and has exactly a $2.76\%$ bad-run chance; the ceiling and that example agree to more than 80 decimal places. Nobody, using only those 7 facts, can promise anything lower. To go further you need more facts. We used Cyclecraft's own high-precision method to compute 12 more tilted averages, and the guarantee fell to $1.58\%$. The true value is about $1.09\%$. The star-shaped version of the machine behaves almost identically.

The gains are largest for rare events. Cyclecraft also asked how likely it is that 10 independent machines together make no forward progress. Their guarantee was $4.3$ in a hundred billion. Ours is $6.2$ in a thousand trillion, about 7,000 times stronger, and within a factor of $9$ of the true value.

One surprise is worth keeping. Some of the extra tilted averages did nothing at all when added alone. Yet after a different one had been added, those same averages became the most useful. Facts can be useless individually and powerful in combination, so choosing which measurement to make next by looking at each one alone is the wrong strategy.

Part three: stop summarising, compute everything

The sandwich squeezes as hard as the facts allow, but 7 facts, or even 19, cannot describe a whole distribution. So we went back to the machine itself. Cyclecraft is a chain of exact fractions: every hopping rate is a fraction, and every rhythm step lasts exactly 2 time units. We extended the machine's state to include the running count, so the computer tracks the machine's position and the count at the same time. Then it pushed all the probability through 10 cycles with interval arithmetic, which carries a guaranteed error bar on every number.

One trick keeps this finite. The count could in principle wander arbitrarily far, so we only track counts between $-40$ and $60$. Any path that wanders outside is thrown away, and we record how much probability was thrown away. Every answer is then bracketed: at least what we kept, at most what we kept plus what we threw away. The thrown-away amount here is about $4\times10^{-58}$, so it changes nothing you could ever measure.

The result is the exact chance of a bad run, $1.092271816412091956957\ldots\%$, certified to more than 50 decimal places. That is 8.4 times smaller than the published guarantee. For 10 machines together, the exact chance is $7.17$ in ten million billion, about 60,000 times smaller than the published $4.3$ in a hundred billion. To keep the joint risk below one in a trillion you need 8 machines, not the 12 the published bound suggests. A side channel that Cyclecraft bounded by $0.13\%$ turns out to have an exact risk of $0.000011\%$, about 11,000 times smaller. To guard against mistakes, the computer also re-derived every average that Cyclecraft had published independently; all of them matched to the last digit an ordinary computer can show.

What is proved, and what is not

Everything marked certified above was checked by the program in exact or interval arithmetic and is a mathematical fact about the stated measurements or the stated machine. In part two, a plain floating-point calculation of the true value served as a test: no certified bound ever fell below it, here or in 24 extra random test boxes. Part three then certified that value exactly. The square-root pattern for pinning down positions is observed, not proved. Nothing here measures a real device. Cyclecraft is a mathematical model, and turning these numbers into hardware claims would need a real calibrated machine.
